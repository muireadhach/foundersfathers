Where the two authorities pull apart

  • The founders' pragmatic discernment ('what works?') vs. the Fathers' transcendent discernment ('what is true before God?')

The Founders 1

The Founderspublic domainlocated in source

But, if we look abroad, shall we not see the most modest, sensible, and virtuous of the common people, almost every hour of their lives, warped and blinded by the same disposition to flatter and deceive themselves? When they think themselves injured by any foible or vice in others, is not this injury always seen through the magnifying end of the perspective? When reminded of any such imperfection in themselves, by which their neighbors or fellow-citizens are sufferers, is not the perspective instantly reversed? Insensible of the beams in our own eyes, are we not quick in discerning motes in those of others?

John Adams, Revolutionary Writings No. ii: To the printers

Scripture anchor Matthew 7:3-5

Why it is here

Young John Adams's essay on self-deceit, 'the spurious offspring of self-love,' with the beam and the mote — Matthew 7:3-5, the research anchor — as his own image for the reversed perspective. A founder writing the cell's diagnostic question as a newspaper essay. Convergence-pair candidate with Angelikoudis on the beam.

Evagrios the Solitary, On Prayer: One Hundred and Fifty-Three Texts Prologue

Scripture anchor Matthew 5:24

Why it is here

Evagrios's own gloss on the cell's phase1 anchor: 'Leave your gift before the altar; first go away and be reconciled with your brother' — then 'rancour darkens the intellect of one who prays.' The passage cites Matthew 5:24 itself. Reconciliation (matrix clause four) and prayer (R5) are shown as one discipline, which is exactly the cell's argument.

The Fathersexcerptlocated in source

If you would follow my advice, cast the beam out of your own eye (cf. Matt. 7:5) — I mean, cast the pretence and self-conceit out of your intellect. And then, when you yourself can see, you will as a consequence legitimately be able to draw out the speck from your brother’s eye, the sin, that is to say, which stealthily entered into him without him being aware of it.

Kallistos Angelikoudis, The ‘Missing Texts’

Scripture anchor John 7:24, Matthew 7:5, Matthew 23:25-26

Why it is here

Matthew 7:3-5 (the research anchor 'assessment begins at home'), cited by the passage and glossed exactly for this cell: the beam is 'pretence and self-conceit,' and seeing clearly is the condition of helping anyone else in the house. Angelikoudis writes to a would-be judge of others; the family that assesses itself first is the same lesson.
